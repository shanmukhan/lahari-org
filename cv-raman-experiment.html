🎨 Illustrative portrait — not a photograph

1888–1970 · Tiruchirappalli & Bangalore, India

C. V. Raman

Discovered that light changes color ever so slightly when it bounces off molecules — a finding that earned India its first Nobel Prize in the sciences.

🌊 A question about the sea

On a ship voyage in 1921, Raman wondered why the deep blue Mediterranean Sea was so blue — was it just reflecting the sky, as scientists assumed, or something else? That question led him toward his discovery.

🔬 The Raman Effect

In 1928, he showed that when light passes through a substance, a tiny fraction of it scatters at a different wavelength — shifted by the exact vibration pattern of the molecules it bounced off.

🏅 Nobel Prize, 1930

He won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930 for this discovery — becoming the first Asian and the first person of color to win a Nobel Prize in any science.

🔍 Reading molecules by their light

Because each molecule scatters light in its own unique way, the "Raman spectrum" acts like a fingerprint — letting scientists identify exactly what substance they're looking at, without touching it.

🧪 A concrete example

Raman spectroscopy is now used to detect counterfeit medicines, identify unknown chemicals at crime scenes, check the authenticity of gemstones, and was even used by NASA's Mars rovers to analyze rock samples millions of miles away — all built on Raman's original 1928 discovery.

🤯 Mind-blowing part: Raman made his discovery using astonishingly simple equipment — mostly sunlight, a prism, and some basic lenses and filters he built himself. No giant laboratory, no expensive machines — just careful observation and a good question about why the sea looked blue.
