🎨 Illustrative portrait, styled after his era — not a photograph

1887–1920 · Erode, India & Cambridge, England

Srinivasa Ramanujan

A largely self-taught mathematician who filled notebooks with original formulas — many of which mathematicians and physicists are still finding new uses for, over a century later.

📓 Teaching himself from one book

With almost no formal training, Ramanujan taught himself advanced mathematics from a single textbook of results, then went far beyond it — rediscovering known theorems and inventing entirely new ones on his own.

✉️ A letter that changed everything

In 1913, he mailed pages of his original formulas to the Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy, who recognized their brilliance instantly and invited him to England to collaborate.

➗ Thousands of original results

He produced nearly 3,900 results — identities, infinite series, and continued fractions — many without formal proofs, simply because he seemed to sense they were true.

📒 The "Lost Notebook"

A notebook of his found decades after his death (called the "Lost Notebook") is still being studied by mathematicians today, revealing formulas connected to modern physics and string theory.

🧮 A concrete example

Ramanujan's formulas for calculating pi converge on the correct answer astonishingly fast — modern computer programs that calculate pi to trillions of digits often use techniques directly built on his 1914 series.

🤯 Mind-blowing part: Ramanujan once described a taxicab numbered 1729 as "very interesting" — it's the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways (1³ + 12³ and 9³ + 10³). Mathematicians now call numbers like it "taxicab numbers" in his honor.
