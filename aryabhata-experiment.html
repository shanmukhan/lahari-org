476–550 CE · Kusumapura (near modern Patna), India

Aryabhata

A mathematician-astronomer who figured out Earth spins on its axis, calculated pi with startling precision, and explained eclipses correctly — over a thousand years before telescopes existed.

📘 His book, the Aryabhatiya

At just 23 years old, he wrote the Aryabhatiya — a compact text covering mathematics and astronomy, written so densely in verse that scholars are still unpacking all of its methods today.

🌍 Earth actually spins

While most of the ancient world believed the sky rotated around a still Earth, Aryabhata proposed that Earth itself rotates on its axis — correctly explaining why the stars appear to move across the night sky.

🥧 A remarkably precise pi

He calculated the value of pi as approximately 3.1416 — accurate to four decimal places — and even noted that it was an approximation, not an exact value, showing real mathematical insight.

🌘 Explaining eclipses correctly

Instead of blaming eclipses on mythical creatures swallowing the Sun or Moon, he correctly explained them as the shadows of Earth and the Moon falling on each other — real astronomy, not folklore.

🛰️ A concrete example

India's first satellite, launched in 1975, was named "Aryabhata" in his honor — a fitting tribute, since satellite orbits are calculated using the same kind of precise mathematics and astronomy he pioneered.

🤯 Mind-blowing part: Aryabhata also worked with place-value notation and the concept behind zero as a placeholder — ideas that, passed through Indian and then Arabic mathematics, eventually became the number system the entire world uses today.
